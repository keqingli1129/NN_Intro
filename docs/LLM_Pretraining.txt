Prediction: The Softmax Function
After all the complex work inside the transformer,  the model outputs a raw score for every single  
token in its vocabulary. This score is called  a loget. Now, these are just raw uncalibrated  
numbers. They aren't probabilities yet. They  can be positive or negative, and they definitely  
don't sum to one. Look at this example. After  the input, the cat sat on the the model might  
output these loit scores. You can see Matt has a  high score of 3.2, 2. Rug is 1.3, floor is 0.5,  
and moon is way down at -2.1. Clearly, Matt is the  front runner, but these scores are messy. To turn  
them into clean probabilities we can actually use,  we apply a crucial function called softmax. Now,  
the softmax formula looks intimidating, but  I promise you, it's just a simple three-step  
recipe. Check out the formula on the screen.  All it's really doing is this. For each token,  
you calculate e to the power of its loget. Then  you divide that result by the sum of all the  
exponentiated logets. This simple operation  guarantees two things. First, every single  
probability will be between 0 and 1. And second,  all the probabilities will add up to exactly 100%.
Let's see it in action. We'll walk through our  simplified example step by step. Look at this  
table. In the first column, we have our tokens.  In the second we have their legit scores. 3.2 for  
Matt, 1.3 for rug and -2.1 for moon. Okay. Step  two, we exponentiate those loia http. So e to the  
power of 3.2 is 24.53. E to the power of 1.3 is  3.67 and e to the power of -2.1 is a tiny.12. The  
sum of all those is 28.32. Now for the final step,  we divide each of those exponentiated scores by  
the total sum. For Matt, we take 24.53 and divide  it by 28.32, which gives us our final probability,  
86.6%. For Rug, it's 3.67 / the total, giving us  13.0%. And for Moon, it's just 0.4%. And what's  
the total? Exactly 100%. And just like that, the  model's messy scores are transformed into a clean,  
understandable probability distribution. We  have an 86.6% chance for Matt, 13% for Rug,  
and a tiny 4% for Moon. The model has officially  made its prediction. We've reached the output

Was that good enough? How do we turn this  into a single error number? That brings

Learning: Cross-Entropy Loss
All right. So, where did we leave off? The  model made its prediction for the input. The  
cat sat on the It gave us this clean probability  distribution. The correct next token was Matt. And  
our model was pretty confident, giving it an 86.6%  chance. But here's the billion dollar question.  
How does the model learn from this? How do we turn  that 86.6% into a signal that says, "Good job,  
but you can do better." We need a way to calculate  a single error number, a score that tells the  
model exactly how wrong it was. This is the job  of the loss function. And for language models,

very low confidence? You'd be very surprised  it was right. This means a high error or high  
loss. The entire goal of training is to adjust  the model's weights to minimize surprise over  
the whole data set. So how do we calculate this  surprise? Check out this formula. This is cross  
entropy loss simplified for our task. The loss  is just the negative log of the probability of  
the correct token. That's it. That is the entire  formula that drives learning. We only care about  
the probability the model assigned to the single  right answer. All the other 50,256 probabilities,
